Refrigeration FAQ

What is a ton of refrigeration (TR)?

One ton of refrigeration is 12,000 BTU/h, or about 3.517 kW. It is the heat needed to melt one short ton (2,000 lb) of ice in 24 hours. So a 10 TR chiller removes roughly 35.2 kW of heat.

How do I calculate superheat?

Superheat = suction line temperature − evaporating (saturation) temperature. Read the suction pressure near the evaporator outlet, convert it to its saturation temperature with a PT chart, then subtract that from the pipe temperature measured at the same point. For blends with glide, use the dew point temperature.

How do I calculate subcooling?

Subcooling = condensing (saturation) temperature − liquid line temperature. Read the liquid line pressure, convert it to saturation temperature, and subtract the liquid line pipe temperature. For blends with glide, use the bubble point temperature.

What is the difference between bubble point and dew point?

Zeotropic blends such as R-407C, R-448A and R-449A do not boil at a single temperature. At a given pressure, liquid starts to boil at the bubble point and the last liquid evaporates at the dew point. The gap between them is the temperature glide. Single-component refrigerants and azeotropes such as R-134a, R-32 and R-507A have no glide.

Gauge pressure vs absolute pressure — which do I use?

Refrigeration gauges read gauge pressure, i.e. relative to the surrounding atmosphere. Absolute pressure = gauge pressure + atmospheric pressure (1.013 bar or 14.7 psi at sea level). PT tables on this page work in either; pick Gauge or Absolute in the Reading selector. At high altitude, atmospheric pressure is lower, so gauge readings read slightly high compared with sea-level tables.

How do I convert COP to EER?

EER = COP × 3.412. For example, a COP of 3.5 equals an EER of about 11.9 BTU/W·h. To get kW/TR, divide 3.517 by the COP: COP 3.5 ≈ 1.005 kW/TR.

How do I calculate chilled water capacity?

Capacity (kW) = flow (L/s) × 4.186 × ΔT (K) for water. In imperial units, BTU/h = 500 × gpm × ΔT (°F). Glycol mixtures have a lower specific heat, so the same flow and ΔT carries less heat.
